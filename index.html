Lesson 1

Adding fractions

You can only add pieces that are the same size. The bottom number (denominator) tells you the piece size, so it has to match first.

Watch it step by step

Steps

  1. Find the LCD.
  2. Rewrite both fractions.
  3. Add the tops, keep the bottom.
  4. Simplify.

Why

The bottom is the piece size. You can only add same-size pieces.

Why it matters

Recipes, measuring, and algebra (x/4 + x/3) all use it.

Same denominator

Add the tops. Keep the bottom.

27+37=57

Different denominators: find a common denominator

  1. Find the least common denominator (LCD), the smallest number both bottoms divide into.
  2. Rewrite each fraction with that denominator. Whatever you do to the bottom, do to the top.
  3. Add the tops, keep the bottom.
  4. Simplify if you can.
14+23→312+812=1112
1/4 cut into twelfths = 3/12
2/3 cut into twelfths = 8/12

Fast method: the butterfly

Good for two fractions when you don't want to hunt for the LCD.

  1. Cross-multiply up the wings: 1×3 = 3 and 4×2 = 8. Add them: 11. That's your top.
  2. Multiply the bottoms (the body): 4×3 = 12. That's your bottom.
  3. Simplify at the end, since this gives a bigger denominator than needed sometimes.

Mixed numbers

Turn them into improper fractions first: multiply the whole number by the bottom, add the top. 213 = 73. Then add like normal.

In the classroom

Whiteboard check: teacher writes 1/2 + 1/3. Everyone writes just the LCD and holds it up. Quick way to see who's stuck on step 1 before moving on.
Fraction strips: fold paper strips into halves, thirds, fourths, sixths. Lay them end to end to "see" that 1/2 + 1/3 fills 5 of the 6 sixths.
Common mistake to call out: 1/2 + 1/3 ≠ 2/5. Adding the bottoms changes the piece size. Ask the class: "Is 2/5 bigger or smaller than 1/2 alone?" It's smaller, so it can't be right.
Lesson 2

Subtracting fractions

Same rules as adding. Match the denominators, then subtract the tops.

Watch it step by step

Steps

  1. Find the LCD.
  2. Borrow 1 whole if the top is too small.
  3. Subtract wholes, then tops.
  4. Simplify.

Why

You can only take away same-size pieces. Borrowing works because 1 whole = 4/4.

Why it matters

Finding what's left or how much longer something is.

Same denominator

58−28=38

Different denominators

  1. Find the LCD.
  2. Rewrite both fractions.
  3. Subtract the tops, keep the bottom.
  4. Simplify.
56−14→1012−312=712

Butterfly works here too, just subtract the wings in order: (5×4) − (6×1) = 14, over 6×4 = 24, which simplifies to 7/12.

Borrowing with mixed numbers

When the top fraction is too small, borrow 1 whole from the whole number and turn it into a fraction.

314−134→254−134=124=112

The borrowed 1 became 4/4, and 1/4 + 4/4 = 5/4. Or skip borrowing: convert both to improper fractions (13/4 − 7/4 = 6/4).

In the classroom

Number line race: draw a 0–2 line split into fourths on the board. Students start at 5/4 and "hop back" 3/4. Where do you land?
Think-pair-share: give 3 1/4 − 1 3/4. One partner borrows, one converts to improper fractions. Compare answers. They should match.
Common mistake: flipping the order to "make it work" (3/4 − 1/4 instead of 1/4 − 3/4). Order matters in subtraction.
Lesson 3

Multiplying fractions

The easiest one. No common denominator needed. Straight across: top times top, bottom times bottom.

Watch it step by step

Steps

  1. Cross-cancel if you can.
  2. Tops × tops.
  3. Bottoms × bottoms.
  4. Simplify.

Why

× means "of." 2/3 × 3/5 is 2/3 of 3/5, so you make new pieces. No common bottom needed.

Why it matters

Halving recipes, discounts, and scaling anything.

23×35=615=25

Cross-cancel first (saves time)

Before you multiply, look diagonally. If a top and a bottom share a factor, divide both by it.

49×38→13×12=16

4 and 8 share 4. 3 and 9 share 3. Smaller numbers, less simplifying at the end. Full cross-canceling lesson →

Whole numbers and mixed numbers

  • Whole number: put it over 1. 5 × 2/3 = 5/1 × 2/3 = 10/3.
  • Mixed number: make it improper first. 1 1/2 × 2/3 = 3/2 × 2/3 = 1.

"Of" means multiply. Half of 3/4 is 1/2 × 3/4 = 3/8.

In the classroom

Area model: draw a square. Shade 2/3 of it in vertical columns, then 3/5 in horizontal rows. The overlap is 6 out of 15 boxes, which is the answer.
Predict first: before solving, ask "Will the answer be bigger or smaller than 2/3?" Multiplying by a fraction less than 1 makes things smaller. This catches a lot of wrong answers.
Lesson 4

Dividing fractions

Turn it into multiplication. Remember it as Keep, Change, Flip.

Watch it step by step

Steps

  1. Keep the first fraction.
  2. Change ÷ to ×.
  3. Flip the second fraction.
  4. Multiply and simplify.

Why

Dividing by a number = multiplying by its flip. ÷ 2 is the same as × 1/2.

Why it matters

"How many fit?" questions, and solving equations in algebra.

  1. Keep the first fraction.
  2. Change ÷ to ×.
  3. Flip the second fraction (its reciprocal).
  4. Multiply straight across and simplify.
34÷25→34×52=158=178

Why flipping works

Dividing asks "how many of these fit?" How many 1/2s fit in 3? Six. And 3 × 2/1 = 6. Flipping the divisor counts how many pieces fit in one whole, then scales it.

Watch out

  • Only flip the second fraction, never the first.
  • Convert mixed numbers to improper fractions before flipping.
  • You can cross-cancel, but only after you've flipped.

In the classroom

Call and response: teacher says "Keep!", class says "Change!", teacher says "Flip!" Do it out loud on the first 3 problems so it sticks.
Pizza question: "You have 3/4 of a pizza. Each person gets 1/8. How many people eat?" Students draw it, then check with Keep Change Flip: 3/4 × 8/1 = 6.
Exit ticket: one problem on a sticky note before leaving: 2/3 ÷ 4/9. (Answer: 3/2 or 1 1/2.)
Lesson 5

Cross-canceling

A shortcut for multiplying (and dividing) fractions. You simplify before you multiply, so you work with small numbers instead of big ones.

Watch it step by step

Steps

  1. Flip first if dividing.
  2. Check both diagonals.
  3. Divide each pair by a shared factor.
  4. Multiply across.

Why

It's just simplifying early. Same answer, smaller numbers.

Why it matters

Fewer mistakes, faster on no-calculator tests.

The steps

  1. Write the problem as multiplication. If it's division, do Keep, Change, Flip first.
  2. Look on the diagonals: the top-left with the bottom-right, and the top-right with the bottom-left.
  3. If a pair shares a factor, divide both numbers by the biggest factor they share (the GCF). Cross them out and write the new numbers.
  4. Multiply straight across with the new numbers. The answer is already simplified.

Example 1

49×38
  • Diagonal 1: 4 (top-left) and 8 (bottom-right) share 4. 4 ÷ 4 = 1, 8 ÷ 4 = 2.
  • Diagonal 2: 3 (top-right) and 9 (bottom-left) share 3. 3 ÷ 3 = 1, 9 ÷ 3 = 3.
13×12=16

Without canceling you'd get 12/72 and have to simplify that. Same answer, more work.

Example 2: only one diagonal cancels

56×97→52×37=1514

9 and 6 share 3, so they become 3 and 2. 5 and 7 share nothing, so leave them alone. That's fine.

Example 3: with division

23÷49→23×94→11×32=32

Flip first, then cancel. 2 and 4 share 2. 9 and 3 share 3.

Rules to remember

  • Only for multiplying (and dividing after you flip). Never cross-cancel when adding or subtracting.
  • Always a top with a bottom. Never cancel two tops or two bottoms with each other across fractions.
  • You can also simplify straight up and down inside one fraction (6/8 → 3/4) before you start.
  • Canceling divides, it doesn't subtract. A number canceled all the way becomes 1, not 0.
  • With a whole number, put it over 1 first: 6 × 5/12 → 6/1 × 5/12 → 1/1 × 5/2 = 5/2.

In the classroom

Draw the X: have students draw a light X across the two fractions before they do anything. The X shows exactly which pairs to check, so nobody cancels two tops.
Race the long way: split the room. Half solves 8/15 × 5/12 the long way, half cross-cancels first. Compare who finishes first and check that both get 2/9.
Spot the error: put up 2/3 + 3/4 with the 3s "canceled" to get 2/1 + 1/4. Ask the class what went wrong. (You can't cancel when adding.)

Practice problems

Random problems. Type your answer in simplest form. Whole numbers go over 1 (like 2/1). Works on a projector for warm-ups too.
